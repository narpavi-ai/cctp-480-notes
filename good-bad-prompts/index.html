Good Prompts vs. Bad Prompts

A companion to Module 2 (Prompt Engineering). Same framework, same examples as the slide deck — compare weak and strong prompts side by side, then paste in your own prompt for a quick self-check.

The framework, quickly

Four questions, answered up front, turn a vague ask into a usable prompt:

Role
Who should the AI act as?
Task
What should the AI do?
Context
What background is relevant?
Output
How should the response look?

Example 1 — Planning a weekend

Weak
What should I do in Edmonton this weekend?

No role, no audience, no format, no timeframe — the AI has to guess everything.

Strong
You are a local Edmonton travel guide. Recommend things to do in Edmonton for a couple visiting for the first time in summer. Provide 5 recommendations. Use bullet points. Include a one-sentence description for each.

Role + Task + Context + Output, all named explicitly.

Example 2 — A business task

Weak
Write a follow-up email to a client.

Which client? Following up on what? What tone? The draft will be generic.

Strong
You are a client-facing account manager at a small consulting firm. Write a follow-up email to a client who hasn't responded to a proposal sent 10 days ago. Keep it warm, brief, and low-pressure — no more than 120 words. End with one clear, easy next step.

Specific role, specific situation, explicit tone and length constraints.

Example 3 — Chain-of-Thought

Direct Prompt
A box holds 4 rows of 6 widgets. How many widgets in 9 boxes?

Model jumps straight to a final number — sounds confident even when the math is wrong.

Chain-of-Thought Prompt
A box holds 4 rows of 6 widgets. How many widgets in 9 boxes? Let's think step by step.

The model works it out — widgets per box, then × 9 boxes — and the reasoning is visible enough to spot-check.
